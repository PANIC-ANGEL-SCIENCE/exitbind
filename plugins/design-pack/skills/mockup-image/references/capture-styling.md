# Frame, light and dress a photographed moment

When the task calls for a reflected self-portrait, use [mirror selfie](mirror-selfie.md) to establish the camera, mirror, hand and room relationship before prompting.

Use for reference-led photographs of people, outfits and spaces, especially when a result has drifted toward a catalogue or staged campaign. Combine with [model direction](model-direction.md), [image production](image-production.md) and the [generation contract](generation-contract.md). These are observation methods, not a universal snapshot style.

## Choose what the photograph needs to show

Choose the subject and framing mode from the current task and actual references before directing a face. A portrait, outfit-led self-portrait, wearer-view detail and object-only photograph carry different evidence. These are useful distinctions, not a mandatory taxonomy or sequence. Inspect the complete source image when deciding its framing: a board thumbnail, browser viewport crop or blocked image edge does not prove that the original excludes the face or object. Keep uncertain source boundaries and capture mode explicit. Classify the crop, face visibility, subject emphasis and capture mechanism separately: an obscured face is not an excluded head, and clothing emphasis does not prove a selfie.

For a portrait or character-led photograph, preserve the requested face, expression and casting evidence. For an outfit-led image, the garment silhouette, layer relationships, fit, movement or construction may be the subject instead. A deliberate neck-down, torso, waist-down or partial-body frame can be complete for that brief; do not automatically widen it to add a head. Choose the cut points around the evidence that matters and keep visible anatomy plausible. Do not invent an off-frame face, gaze, exact age or identity assessment to fill a checklist.

Distinguish a mirror-mediated outfit image from a downward wearer view, a detail photographed by someone else, and an uncertain source. A mirror mode needs coherent reflected body/phone/room geometry where visible, not a compulsory full face or visible mirror border. A downward wearer view can show clothing, footwear, hands or surroundings without a mirror; the capturing phone may be outside the frame. Do not insert a second phone, face or reflection merely to prove the word selfie. Keep only the body and scene evidence needed to support the chosen view.

For an object-only photograph, make the selected object or collection the visual subject. Choose whole-object, flat-lay, held/in-use, isolated, contextual still-life or detail framing according to the actual task. A hand may support scale or use when requested, but no face, full person, hand or elaborate room is compulsory. Preserve meaningful object count, boundaries, orientation, arrangement, material/light and required markings. A plain product background and an ordinary lived surface are different valid choices; do not turn every source into a catalogue shot or every item into a personal snapshot. A printed portrait can be the content of an object without making the overall photograph a character portrait.

Review the selected mode and required visible evidence independently. A face intentionally outside an outfit-only brief is not a casting failure; face or expression checks do not apply when they are neither requested nor visible. Conversely, hiding a face cannot solve an unresolved portrait requirement, and calling something a detail view cannot excuse missing required garment or object evidence. A polished face does not rescue the wrong framing or a lost item. Compare the actual crop at final use size and preserve the latest accepted composition during a narrow repair unless the user reopens it.

## Vary body view and action for a reason

Treat front, profile, three-quarter, back, partial-body and deliberately obscured-face views as task-conditioned choices. A back view can foreground garment fall, carrying or movement; a side view can reveal depth, layering or attention away from the camera. Outfit-led framing can prioritize how clothes are worn without supplying a portrait. These are possible purposes, not fixed meanings. Choose the view that serves the current brief and inspected relationships, not a permanent front-facing mirror pose or a mandatory rotation through every angle.

Plan body orientation and camera position together. Specify the support or motion, hands' actual task, garment response and what the crop must reveal. Distinguish a profile face from a torso turned sideways, and an obscured face from a back view. Keep visible body/hand geometry plausible and mirror relationships coherent only when the selected capture mode actually uses a mirror. Do not twist the body or add an extra face/phone to show contradictory views at once.

Treat face visibility as a deliberate evidence choice. Hair, a hand, a phone, a turned head, shadow or framing can reduce facial detail when supported, without compulsory blur or a hidden identity story. Do not describe unseen eyes, mouth or expression as verified. Preserve any face, tattoo, accessory or garment feature that the current task requires to be visible; concealment cannot close an unresolved requirement. Conversely, do not widen a valid back or outfit-led view to satisfy irrelevant facial checks. A new character's fictional adult brief can remain explicit without claiming exact age verification from a face that is not visible.

Compare the actual result with the prior image when variation is requested: body/action, viewpoint and garment interaction should change in the intended ways. Changing labels or adding props to the same stance is not enough. One strong different view can satisfy one requested draft; do not produce an unrequested contact sheet. Keep authorized constants and assess pose, framing and expression applicability separately.

## Compose two or more people as a shared moment

When the task calls for a group, plan the actual requested count and each person's role in the frame; two people already form a multi-person composition. Do not turn every character request into a group or assume a minimum of three. A reference section about friends supplies visual relationships, not proof that the pictured people are friends, partners or relatives. Fictional relationships can be authored when useful and authorized, but must remain invented context.

Arrange people through their attention, spacing, depth, support and interaction rather than a row of independent portraits. Different people can face different directions, respond to each other or attend elsewhere; synchronized gaze and matching expressions are optional. Preserve individual silhouettes, styling and gestures without compulsory demographic contrast or a personality assigned from appearance. Shared clothing or a quiet expression is not itself duplication. Avoid repeated faces, copied poses or interchangeable accessories when distinct individuals are required.

Trace visible limbs and contact across overlaps: which arm belongs to whom, where weight is supported, what a hand touches, and how clothing compresses or casts shadow. Partial people and obscured faces can fit the selected frame, but cannot conceal an unresolved required person or item. Check scale, perspective, light and physical separation across the same space. In reflected scenes, map each actual person to their reflection instead of counting a reflection as an extra person. Keep photographer, phone-holder and mirror roles plausible; do not invent a second camera merely to explain the frame.

Review count, individual distinction, interaction/contact and required visibility separately at whole-frame and use scales. A convincing pair does not satisfy a required third person, and a technically correct count does not establish a shared moment. Do not claim real friendship or group identity from the result. Choose one coherent group composition when requested, not an unrequested collection of solo and group variations.

## Read the frame before adding a camera label

Inspect actual source pixels, including useful full images rather than only a board grid. Separate recurring relationships from exceptions and missing coverage. Keep source identity, captures and findings in the private project. Do not infer device, focal length, exposure settings or spontaneous behavior from appearance.

Compare the camera's height, distance and angle with the subject: subject scale and position, near-to-far size change, headroom, cut limbs or garment edges, foreground obstruction, mirror/phone involvement, and the background's share of attention. Note what the photographer seems to be attending to, while keeping that interpretation separate from visible evidence. A cropped face, centered person, tilted frame or full-body view is neither required nor automatically wrong.

For an informal or participant-camera brief, test whether the image has silently become a product display: isolated centered subject, complete outfit arranged for inspection, empty symmetrical setting, equal sharpness and flattering light across every surface. Diagnose the combined relationship, not a single forbidden feature. Deliberate pose and clear detail can still fit a personal photograph. An explicit catalogue brief can require those same choices.

Choose a coherent camera position and moment from the inspected relationships. Preserve useful background involvement or edge interruptions when they serve the brief; do not insert clutter, random tilt or accidental blur as authenticity tokens. When product visibility conflicts with the captured moment, use another authorized view or receiving crop for necessary detail instead of forcing every garment into one display pose. Keep required evidence visible somewhere appropriate and preserve an approved layout within the task's change scope.

## Infer lighting cautiously from visible relationships

Read light independently from room style, wardrobe and color palette. Compare lit planes, shadow direction and edge softness, face-to-background brightness, near-to-far falloff, reflections and catchlights, local color casts, highlight clipping and shadow detail. Distinguish visible evidence from a possible light source; a bright face alone does not prove flash, and an interior window does not prove it is the dominant source. Do not invent camera metadata or exact color temperature.

Separate person/outfit references from spatial references by source role. A room reference may guide the position of a bright window, darker recesses or practical lights without supplying a facial-lighting recipe. Transfer the selected lighting relationship into the new subject's actual position; do not paste an evenly lit person onto a darker room. Check occlusion, contact shadow, reflected light and reflections for the same plausible arrangement.

Choose one coherent lighting situation from the reference variation rather than combining every source into one frame. Window daylight, a local night-time lamp and a near-camera hard source can be alternatives; they need not all appear. Multiple sources are valid when their positions, contributions and mixed color form a consistent scene. Preserve useful variation across a series instead of averaging it into one generic lighting setup.

For an everyday-camera brief, do not automatically add studio fill, rim light, soft beauty illumination, neutral white balance or an evenly exposed background. Where supported, retain the observed difference between a shaded subject and a bright opening, a nearby hard source and darker distance, or differently colored ambient sources. Soft window light, clean daylight and even exposure remain valid when supported; hard flash, crushed blacks and mixed color are not mandatory phone-photo cues. Exposure limits should follow the scene, not a damage filter. Do not fake poor quality with grain, haze, underexposure or blown highlights merely to assert phone capture.

Translate only the decisive observations into the prompt, then inspect their effect in the returned photo and receiving crop. Assess lighting fit separately from texture: grain removal cannot fix studio staging, and a device label cannot fix an incompatible light arrangement. Keep enough face, material and product evidence for the task without flattening every shadow.

## Separate illumination, exposure and capture response

For a renewed lighting request, reopen relevant current source images rather than repeating an earlier mood label. Compare useful whole scenes and details across the supported range, noting coverage and counterexamples. Distinguish three things: light reaching the scene, the surfaces reflecting it, and the recorded tonal/color response. A black garment does not prove low illumination, a pale wall does not prove overexposure, and a bright window does not reveal a measured lighting ratio.

Observe source direction and apparent size, shadow transitions, falloff and local color first. Then compare where highlights retain detail or appear clipped, whether shaded face/cloth detail remains, and how the subject differs from near and far background. Relative brightness visible in the image is not measured illuminance. Do not invent lux, ISO, shutter, aperture or sensor characteristics from pixels. If reliable metadata is available, identify its source separately; it still does not prove the whole scene's lighting setup.

Assess capture response at native/detail and receiving scales. Note only supported noise or color mottling, fine-detail loss, sharpening halos, compression, and directional subject/camera blur. Similar artifacts can come from processing, resizing or recompression; visible grain does not establish high ISO, and a clean low-light picture does not prove studio lighting. Do not manufacture a sensitivity value or apply uniform fake grain to make the image feel photographic.

Choose a coherent light and exposure intention for the new scene: which surfaces receive the main light, which stay shaded, what highlights or face/material detail matter, and how brightness falls through the space. Choose capture texture separately, with restrained detail appropriate to the reference and final scale. A high-contrast or dim reference need not be copied as global darkness; clean light and detailed shadows remain valid. Avoid automatically lifting every shadow, neutralizing all local color, adding beauty fill or balancing every object to equal brightness.

Carry a few decisive observations into production, not a stack of contradictory camera adjectives or invented settings. Reinspect face, hands, clothing, object surfaces and room under the same arrangement. Glossy supporting objects may add glare or hide their design; check that visibility separately rather than giving them a private studio light. Test light arrangement, exposure fit, capture response and required evidence independently. Noise removal cannot repair an incompatible exposure relationship, and darker exposure cannot repair a staged camera position.

## Analyze clothing as worn relationships

Study the outfit as a system: relative upper/lower volume and length, fitted versus loose areas, waist placement, layer lengths and openings, closures, sleeve and hem bunching, material weight or sheen, footwear bulk and accessory position. Relate these to body position, movement, light and crop. State which details are actually visible; a thumbnail or dark area cannot establish fiber, brand, product construction or hidden layers.

Transfer a few supported relationships, not a shopping list or a brand label. A narrow upper silhouette over loose lower volume, an uneven layer opening or an accessory interrupting a line can guide a new outfit without copying an exact ensemble. Do not convert one user's board into universal dark clothing, distressed styling, a fixed silhouette or a profession/culture uniform. Include counterexamples that matter to the current direction; unusual items alone do not create a coherent personal style.

Respect the task's wardrobe authority. If garments, construction or identity are fixed, preserve them and vary only authorized fit, wear, camera or scene relationships. If new outfits are requested, vary their connected proportions and materials rather than repeating a generic outfit with different accessories. Do not add or remove clothing, logos, products or styling solely to rescue another failed axis. Factual product evidence needs supplied assets and verified details; generated clothing remains illustrative.

## Preserve the fashion relationships that distinguish the sources

Before choosing a new outfit or repairing a rejected one, identify the visible relationships that make the selected references specific. Color, a broad garment category and a fit adjective are not enough when the brief depends on distinctive fashion. Compare silhouette and relative lengths; where the waist/hip is exposed or hidden; how layers cross, hang or open; the path and attachment of panels, seams and closures; and differences in material surface, weight and drape. A flat garment view can clarify cut and boundaries, while a worn view reveals gravity, tension and proportion. Do not infer exact fiber, price, brand authenticity or unseen pattern construction from appearance.

Choose a small coherent set of defining relationships for this task and state what may vary. Keep an observation-to-output comparison for each consequential choice: what is visibly distinctive, how it will be reinterpreted, and where it must remain observable. Do not require a fixed number of details, copy every item or make the user's private wardrobe a universal preset. A simpler source or explicitly simpler brief can legitimately yield a simple outfit.

Preserve those relationships through the actual prompt and returned image, not just the research notes. Do not reduce a shaped layered garment to a color plus ordinary jacket, or a materially distinct lower silhouette to generic loose trousers. A change of fictional person, age, gender presentation, occupation or orientation does not itself authorize making the styling more conventional. Those attributes do not determine clothing taste. Vary the outfit within the requested direction rather than repeating identical garments or replacing the reference character with a social stereotype.

When feedback says details look cheap, imitative or arbitrary, diagnose the visible defect before changing the wardrobe. Inspect whether panel edges and closures agree, gathers have coherent support, volume and folds follow the apparent material and body position, and trim integrates with the garment. These are illustrative visual checks, not proof of physical manufacturability or retail value. Fix the failed construction/material relationship without deleting the distinctive silhouette, layering or contrast the brief still needs. Sparse detail is not automatically better or more expensive. Decoration can be intentional; not every detail needs an invented utilitarian function. Avoid random seams, straps, hardware or uniform crumpling as substitutes for a coherent cut.

Check that posing, layer length and framing do not hide the very feature chosen from the reference. If a top, bag, hand or crop obscures required waist/hip, sleeve or hem evidence, adjust the authorized pose, layer relation or view while preserving a believable photograph. Do not turn every outfit into a catalogue diagram. A companion detail can support a requirement, but cannot excuse losing the central fashion direction from the main image.

Reopen the selected source beside the output at comparable whole-frame and detail scales. Compare silhouette first, then cut/layer/material relationships and how they are worn. Ask what remains specific after setting aside color labels, facial appeal and phone texture. If the outfit could be any generic substitute and the defining source relationships disappeared, keep reference fidelity in rework even when the image is clean, young-looking or technically plausible. Record deliberate departures and their authorization. A plausible new outfit is not automatically a faithful interpretation of the selected references.

## Carry material and construction through the whole garment

When a garment reads as a model, shell or costume rather than worn material, diagnose visible behavior before adding texture. Compare the current output with relevant source details at useful scales: apparent edge thickness, bending and crease transitions, supported versus free-hanging areas, compression at joints or closures, and highlights/shadows across different surfaces. Separate a material-response defect from implausible cut, unsupported folds or lighting. Uniform grain, repeated wrinkles, global sharpening or extra gloss cannot establish fabric weight. Heavy-looking material need not be inflated or rigid, and soft-looking material need not melt around the body. These observations do not verify actual fiber, mass or manufacturability.

Trace the selected garment from its support points to its finish. For trousers, inspect waist/hip attachment and layer overlap, the changing volume through thigh and knee, seam or panel paths, lower-leg shape and the hem's relation to footwear or floor. Other garments need their own relevant path. Check that a feature continues, joins or terminates coherently, rather than requiring every seam to run the whole length. A detailed upper section and quieter lower section can be intentional; the failure is lost structural/material logic, not unequal decoration density.

Read the actual limits of each reference. A cropped product image cannot establish its unseen hem or back. Use a source that shows the required finish, an explicit specification, or a clearly identified illustrative design decision within scope. Do not describe invented stitching, lining, fasteners or unseen construction as observed evidence. When a new detail is authorized, make it belong to the existing cut, thickness, openings and use instead of attaching arbitrary trim. An ankle opening, finished edge or visible fastening is one possible solution, not a universal requirement.

Resolve contact and finishing at the final use scale. A hem can break, stack, skim, open or overlap footwear when the chosen cut, length and material support it. Look for plausible asymmetric compression, a readable edge where visible, contact shadows, and continuity of material response into the lowest region. Do not require a rigid cuff, perfectly symmetrical pooling or a clean full view of both hems. Occlusion may be honest, but it cannot be used to hide an unresolved detail the task requires. More tiny stitches are not evidence of better construction; only describe or verify detail the actual output resolves.

For a narrow material or hem correction, preserve the accepted subject, upper styling, garment identity, hip arrangement, camera and light within the current scope. Compare changed and protected regions after editing, including the transitions immediately above the repair. Do not trade a believable hem for a redesigned outfit or a new face. Review material response and whole-garment continuity independently: believable surface texture can coexist with a disconnected lower leg, while a coherent seam path can still look molded. Keep either required failure in rework rather than treating decorative detail as completion.

## Keep tattoos and accessories attached to the photographed body

Inspect relevant actual details before transferring a mark, piercing or accessory relationship. Record only resolved shape, location, scale, attachment, layering and surface response; do not infer a real person's motives, identity, pain, healing history or exact material. When the task permits original fictional details, choose their forms and meanings under [image production](image-production.md), without copying an identifiable person's tattoo as personal biography. No mark or accessory is compulsory and more items do not automatically create variety.

Make a required tattoo follow the same skin, body orientation and capture as its placement. Check foreshortening around a curved arm or torso, the mark's relation to joints, visible stretch or compression under the chosen pose, occlusion and local light/texture. Ink should not float as a flat sticker, migrate to another limb, repeat mechanically or keep an impossible front-facing orientation. A reflected view may reverse the graphic. Preserve the intended design where the output resolves it without asserting exact hidden paths or tiny text. Do not add invented scars, inflammation or conspicuous skin damage to prove realism.

For piercings and accessories, choose task-relevant differences in form, scale, material, position, finish and combination rather than piling on a standard set. Inspect the visible attachment: a post, loop, clasp, strap, clip or supported resting point should plausibly belong to the body, hair or garment. Check which ear/side or body region is actually visible, including reflection uncertainty, before claiming placement. Metal, translucent material and textile respond differently to light and contact. Avoid floating rings, fused jewelry, duplicate chains or a bag strap passing through a shoulder. Do not force exposure of every small item; verify only what the scene makes visible and keep required detail unresolved when it cannot be inspected.

For nails and rings, compare the actual nail shape/length/finish and which fingers carry which objects when the source resolves them. Let hand activity, grip and viewing angle constrain the detail; do not multiply fingers or enlarge jewelry to display it. A bare nail, worn polish, short practical shape or elaborate treatment can each be chosen, not an identity rule. Do not infer a brand, exact product or durable material from appearance. Keep clothing and exposure within the brief rather than undressing a character merely to display every tattoo or accessory.

## Resolve garment structure before a color or category substitution

When a selected garment is rejected as generic, inspect the relevant source in whole-outfit view and resolved garment detail before another prompt. A color match and a category name do not preserve a designed silhouette. Describe the visible outline, volume distribution and lengths first, then the cut, support and material behavior that plausibly create them. Separate a seam, opening, overlay and pose-induced fold where the pixels allow it; when they do not, retain uncertainty rather than claiming a sewing pattern or fiber specification. Keep the observations and chosen source identities in the private project, not this reusable pack.

Create a short task-local construction brief for the chosen garment: where its weight is supported, how volume travels from that support to the end, which closure or overlapping piece changes that volume, where the body bends, and how the edge meets the adjacent garment, footwear or ground. For trousers this may run from waist/hip through thigh and knee to the hem. The method also applies to other garments without requiring trousers or visible legs. Identify the few source relationships that must survive, the authorized original changes and the exact generic fallback already rejected. Resolve this brief before generation; elaborate prose written after the output cannot supply missing construction.

Treat unusual features as connected garment behavior, not scattered ornaments. An overlapping piece must have a plausible attachment and fall; a shaped or articulated region must agree with the standing or bent limb; an opening must terminate coherently rather than become an unrelated line; a panel must remain distinguishable from shadow without turning into a rigid toy shell. These are checks, not instructions to add wraps, panels, zippers, pleats or asymmetry to every garment. Choose only structures supported by the selected reference or an explicitly authorized original design. Simple straight trousers can be correct for a different brief; a request for a more distinctive garment does not justify arbitrary hardware, luxury claims or imitation brand labels.

Carry the construction brief into the actual supported image input and compact prompt. When changing a garment type, choose the new structure deliberately instead of silently falling back to a familiar tailored trouser in the requested hue. Retain the current character, pose, upper styling and camera unless reopened. Reconcile the changed cut with the existing support, bend and footwear contact rather than pasting a new flat silhouette over the same body. If a reference is inaccessible or details unresolved, record the limitation and do not claim direct reference fidelity.

Review the generated garment in two separate passes: outline/volume at whole-frame and intended-use size, then visible construction/material/edge continuity at native detail. Compare it with the chosen source relationships and the rejected fallback, not only the prompt. Does the garment still have the required shape when color and styling labels are ignored? Does the visible cut explain the volume without invented decorative noise? Check a local repair's transitions and preserved regions. Keep either required failure in rework even if material realism, anatomy or palette passes. Structural plausibility is not proof of manufacture, exact source reproduction, price tier or user approval.

## Preserve outfit intent as a coupled relationship

When the brief or feedback names a style context or mood, inspect the exact selected image and current output rather than reducing the source to a palette. Read material response and apparent structure, garment shape and length, layer order/openings, closure/hardware scale, wear, accessories and the way the person carries them together. Distinguish what is visibly observed, what the user explicitly specifies and what is your working interpretation. A user-specified material or intended mood is a task constraint even when a photograph alone cannot prove fiber or cultural provenance. Do not infer a real person's membership, beliefs, profession or life story from the look.

State the task's outfit intent in a short project-local relationship, then identify the few combinations that make it recognizable. A structured, light-catching surface over a soft underlayer can carry a different intention from loosely knitted surfaces in the same colors; changed edge stiffness, layer length or hardware emphasis can alter the whole reading. These are diagnostic examples, not mandatory garments or a universal style formula. The selected source and latest brief decide which relationships matter and what can vary. A style label alone is not an actionable prompt, and one label can support many different outfits.

Treat color, material, silhouette and styling context as mutually dependent choices. Do not silently substitute a knit for a structured surface, a draped volume for a narrow line, or a generic accessory for a defining proportion merely because the hue remains similar. Such changes may be legitimate when authorized, but matching the color cannot establish that the intended outfit survived. Likewise, a more coherent palette does not compensate for lost tactile contrast or a different wearing language. Preserve the meaningful relationships through garment selection, not only in a written mood description.

Reinterpret deliberately instead of copying every item. Choose which source relationships to retain, which to adapt to the current body/action and which to leave behind. The source's unseen lower body, back or small details remain unknown; design authorized missing regions consistently and label them as original decisions rather than claimed source facts. A mood is not permission to add a standard bundle of symbols, props, distress, hardware or exaggerated expression. Ordinary pieces can belong to a strong direction through cut, material and wear; more conspicuous pieces do not automatically make it faithful.

Compare the actual outfit against the inspected source at whole-frame and useful material/layer detail scales. Ask whether the relationship still reads when color names and the mood label are set aside. Use a task-specific counterfactual when helpful: if an intended material were replaced by an unrelated one of the same color, would the meaning change? If yes, treat that material relationship as consequential, not interchangeable. Record deliberate departures and unresolved ambiguity. Keep outfit-intent fit in rework when the key relationship disappears even if palette, individual item quality and anatomy pass. This is a judgment against the chosen brief, not an objective classifier for cultural authenticity.

## Vary outfit color through relationships, not random swaps

Resolve the coupled outfit intent above before selecting a palette; more color variety must not erase the material, silhouette or context the user is asking for. When a user requests broader color combinations, reopen the relevant current references and compare complete worn outfits as well as isolated items. Record actual dominant and secondary color areas, warm/cool or value contrast, saturated accents, repetition and where color meets skin, hair, accessories and the setting. Keep observed image color distinct from verified product color: local illumination, reflection and processing can change its appearance. A dark material does not necessarily have the same hue in every lit plane.

Choose a coherent task-specific palette with a reason in the fictional person's taste or the current situation, while retaining required garment construction and material relationships. Colored inner layers, larger light areas, mixed neutrals, complementary or closely related hues are options, not required recipes. Let material sheen, texture, translucency and layer size affect the relationship. Do not average varied references into default black/grey, use demographic identity or profession as a palette rule, or sprinkle arbitrary bright accents merely to claim diversity. Monochrome remains valid when requested or specifically supported.

Apply color changes only to the authorized clothing/accessory scope; do not silently recolor skin, a protected tattoo, interface or background. Review the actual outfit at whole-frame and use size under the chosen light: does the combination remain distinct and coherent, and do the intended cut/layer/material relationships survive? One image needs one chosen combination, not every observed color. If a series is authorized, compare meaningful palette differences without changing protected identity or making a uniform color-swapped lineup.

## Rebuild coupled choices when the whole scene is rejected

Use [image production](image-production.md) to distinguish a whole reset from a local repair. An approved face, outfit or setting is not permanently protected after the user explicitly asks to replace it. Conversely, preserve requirements the user has not reopened. Build the new subject, worn outfit, space and camera as one relationship, using actual section-specific evidence through [source roles](../../design-pack/references/reference-roles.md). Do not turn the most recent look, room type or phone pose into the next default.

For an apparent face/body mismatch, use [model direction](model-direction.md) to inspect one subject across head, neck, shoulders and the supported body. Read perspective, hair/clothing volume and pose-related occlusion before changing proportions. Compare light, color and photographic detail across the transition without equalizing naturally shaded and lit skin. Preserve the selected side/back or partially seen view during a bounded repair; a front-facing replacement or extra blur is not evidence of continuity.

## Compare the actual result on separate axes

At whole-frame, intended-use and relevant detail scales, compare framing/moment, lighting, styling/wear, expression and capture texture separately against the inspected sources and brief. Recheck every receiving crop: an environmental photo can become a catalogue portrait when cropped tightly, while a useful garment relationship may disappear at phone size. Compare changed and protected regions after revisions.

Write the observable mismatch and a narrow repair. If framing still reads as staged product display for an informal brief, keep it in rework even when face, outfit and texture pass. If room lighting works but the person is lit incompatibly, or new styling breaks a fixed product requirement, those remain separate failures. A more casual pose, noisier surface or compliant prompt cannot close them. Explicit commercial staging, bright clean rooms and simple outfits remain valid for tasks that request them.

Retain actual guidance reads, submitted call/input records, native outputs and their review when traceability matters. Distinguish manual use of local guidance from host activation, and neither from a causal quality claim. A package version, written receipt or structural test cannot prove the maker applied this method or that a viewer will prefer the result.
