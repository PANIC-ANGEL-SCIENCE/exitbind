# From vague request to actionable brief

The designer's job is to make the ambiguity discussable, not to force the client to become a designer. Begin with the client's words and the situation behind them. Explain your interpretation in ordinary language before discussing implementation.

## 1. Establish the real problem

Extract what is already known: audience, context, task, intended feeling, business or communication outcome, current artifact, channels, deadline, ownership, and things that must survive. Review supplied references and project material before asking for facts already present.

Use five explicit evidence labels:

- **User-stated:** their actual requirement, with scope and date.
- **Observed:** visible or measured in the current artifact; name the evidence.
- **Hypothesis:** a plausible explanation to test.
- **Proposed:** a design choice you recommend, not yet a client preference.
- **Confirmed:** an explicit decision or a choice made within delegated authority; record which.

If nothing is available, write a provisional brief with honest unknowns. Do not fabricate a brand story or pretend a generated persona is user research.

## 2. Translate adjectives into testable implications

| Client phrase | Possible meanings | Useful discriminator | Low-cost test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| “Make it premium” | Careful editorial craft; trustworthy service; conspicuous luxury | “Should it feel quietly assured or expressive and collectible?” | Two styleframes with the same real content; compare hierarchy and evidence of quality |
| “Clean but exciting” | Easier scanning with a memorable moment | “Which action must stay effortless, and where can we invite exploration?” | Quiet task path plus one expressive area; test both |
| “More alive” | Responsive feedback; richer imagery; changing content | “Is the problem that clicks feel unresponsive, or that the page feels static?” | One state transition versus one content/composition alternative |
| “Like this reference” | Rhythm, density, material, navigation, mood or literal fidelity | Annotate what you think matters and what should not transfer | Proportional sketch and a short interpretation; preserve identity separately |
| “It feels wrong” | Misaligned hierarchy, tone, audience, familiarity or function | Ask for the first moment it stops feeling right, and what should be noticed first | Before/after at that exact moment |
| “Make it pop” | Stronger priority, contrast, differentiation or feedback | “What should a first-time visitor notice or do first?” | Rebalance one hierarchy rather than increasing every accent |

These are competing interpretations, not definitions. Use the user's language; never equate “professional” with muted colors or “creative” with clutter.

## 3. Choose the right clarification effort

**Low consequence and reversible:** State a one-line reading and proceed. Example: “I'll interpret ‘cleaner’ as making the primary action easier to find while keeping the current visual character.”

**Materially different directions or costly commitment:** Present 2–3 alternatives with meaningful differences, a recommendation, rationale, and change cost. Ask 1–3 high-value questions in a round, not a long questionnaire. Use visual comparisons where appearance is the uncertainty. Don't ask users to select technical parameters they cannot evaluate.

**Unresolved authority, private data, budget or essential facts:** Ask the specific missing question and continue independent work. No amount of elapsed time supplies an answer.

**Explicitly delegated creative judgment:** Choose a direction within stated constraints and keep working. Record the decision as designer-selected under delegation. Do not create repeated approval gates.

**User requests co-design:** Agree the consequential checkpoints: brief, direction and representative prototype. Technical craft decisions normally stay with the designer. Reuse prior approvals and do not restart intake after each iteration.

Prioritize questions by: impact on outcome × uncertainty × cost of changing late. If two answers lead to the same implementation, the question is probably unnecessary.

## 4. Build a defensible direction

Each direction card must include:

1. A one-sentence concept anchored in the client's actual subject.
2. What the audience should understand, feel and do.
3. Content order and visual/interaction implications.
4. Reference observations and which properties transfer.
5. Constraints, cost, accessibility and maintenance tradeoffs.
6. The smallest prototype and evidence that could disprove the choice.

Compare alternatives against the brief, not arbitrary “taste scores.” All candidates should use comparable content and fidelity. Identify hard constraints first; a beautiful option that hides the required action is not a winner. An unfamiliar interaction may be intentional if the audience and context support it, but provide an accessible route and explain the cost.

## 5. Converge and handle feedback

Read back the chosen direction with a concrete example, preservation conditions and acceptance checks. Record assumptions that remain open. Separate functional acceptance from creative preference; neither replaces the other.

When feedback arrives, restate the desired effect, locate it on the artifact, identify the underlying variable, propose the smallest coherent change, and check its impact elsewhere. “Bigger logo” may mean “the brand is not identifiable”; test hierarchy before blindly enlarging every element. If the user explicitly requires the larger logo, honor it and show the resulting tradeoff.

For conflicting stakeholders, name the conflict and decision owner. Do not blend incompatible requests into a compromise nobody asked for. For contradictory requirements, offer feasible alternatives; never silently drop one.

Record changes in a small decision log: date, evidence, decision, scope, reason, alternatives, affected artifacts and checks to rerun. A changed brief can invalidate old evidence.

## Example: ambiguous → concrete

Request: “Our appointment page should feel more premium but not cold.”

Known: existing service descriptions and booking flow must remain; mobile visitors are the main audience. Unknown: whether “premium” means intimacy or clinical assurance.

Interpretation: “Make the service feel considered and welcoming, while keeping availability and booking easy to find.”

Alternatives: **Human editorial** uses staff imagery and story-led sections; **Calm precision** foregrounds process, credentials and availability. Recommend the first only if usable, licensed human imagery exists; otherwise use the second with warm, concrete copy. No invented staff portraits presented as real.

Acceptance: a visitor can identify the service, find availability, understand price and cancellation terms, and complete or exit booking by keyboard and touch; the chosen tonal qualities are visible in the representative prototype. These are proposed checks, not measured conversion improvement.
