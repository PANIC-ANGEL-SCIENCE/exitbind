# Motion, video, narrative and sound

Choose the output first: interactive UI motion, explanatory animation, launch film, editorial video, spatial experience or audio identity. A still, storyboard, cue sheet and final render are different deliverables. Do not claim the last when only the first exists.

## Brief and story

Define audience, message, intended action, channel, aspect ratios, duration, language, audio context and available assets. Gather real product facts and permitted material. Create a narrative spine: setup, development, evidence/reveal and resolution. A product film must not promise behavior the product lacks.

Write a timed storyboard with shot ID, purpose, duration or frame range, visual state, copy, camera, transition, audio cue, asset provenance and accessibility equivalent. Build styleframes for the key moments before rendering a whole sequence. Hold key information long enough to read in the target context; determine durations from content and audience, not a universal “premium” timing table.

## Motion decisions

For each motion, state its purpose: feedback, orientation, continuity, explanation or expression. Frequent tasks need immediate response and should not wait for decorative animation. Choose easing and duration from distance, object behavior, input and perceptual intent. Avoid unintended motion from animating unrelated properties. Preserve interruptibility when repeated input is expected.

For films or procedural animation, define a reproducible timebase, seed any randomness, resolve fonts/assets before capture, and make a representative low-cost draft first. Inspect shot boundaries, first/last frames, timing and composition; representative stills alone cannot reveal every one-frame flash or continuity defect. Full playback remains necessary.

Beat alignment is a creative choice, not a universal rule. If used, inspect the actual audio onset and rhythm; an estimated BPM grid is not evidence of perceptual synchronization. Distinguish a visual cut, effect onset and audible peak. Preserve room for silence and stillness.

## Sound as design

Define event meaning, emotional tone, timbre, duration, priority and repetition behavior. Make a cue sheet tied to states or shots. Avoid making every interaction sound; consider fatigue and interruption. Provide mute/volume control where relevant and a visual or textual alternative for essential information.

Record license and provenance for every recording, music track, voice, sample and generated asset. A repository's code license does not license its demo soundtrack. Do not assume a public voice can be impersonated. Choose channel-appropriate delivery levels and check clipping, dynamics, frequency masking, intelligibility and cue synchronization.

Listen to the actual mix when tools permit. Waveform or loudness measurements support but do not replace listening. If playback is unavailable, state that limit and deliver a cue/mix specification without calling it acoustically approved.

## Validation and handoff

Check reduced-motion alternatives in interactive contexts; readable captions/transcripts and relevant audio description; no essential message communicated only by sound, movement or color; flash risks; timing at actual playback speed; aspect-ratio crops; compression and device playback. Screen-readability, dialogue and sound effects should be considered together.

Deliver editable source/timeline, brief, storyboard, asset/rights ledger, audio stems when in scope, exports with codec/resolution/frame rate/duration, caption files, static fallback and evidence. Identify which rendering/listening operations actually ran. No render engine is bundled or required for the planning workflow.
